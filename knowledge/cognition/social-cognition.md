# Social Cognition

## Why

A language model writes dialogue like a cooperative information exchange:
every question gets its answer, every motive is legible, every social
interaction converges on clarity. Human social life is nothing like that.
People evade, perform politeness, misunderstand, pursue hidden agendas, and
answer the question they *wish* had been asked. And crucially, the POV
character does not have access to the other person's actual intention — only
to their own reading of it.

## Social misalignment

Dialogue may involve avoidance, indirect answers, hidden agendas, politeness
as camouflage, misunderstanding, deliberate ambiguity, withholding, or
answering a different implied question. The POV narrator represents **what
the POV character thinks is happening** — never the other person's actual
intention revealed as fact.

## Non-cooperative dialogue (character- and context-dependent)

Human dialogue permits:

- question → partial answer
- question → avoidance
- question → counter-question
- question → topic shift
- question → misunderstanding
- question → emotional response instead of an answer
- question → socially polite non-answer

Do not make every character answer exactly what they are asked. But do not
randomize evasion either: each misalignment must follow from the character's
goals, fears, habits, or the social context.

## Different models of the same character

Character A may believe "B is confident."
Character C may believe "B is arrogant."
B may believe "I am merely being practical."
Story truth may be different again.

Do not collapse these into one canonical psychological description during
narration. Each POV renders others through its own interpretive habits.

## Social perception in narration

Narration should layer, without flattening:

- what the POV character sees (observation)
- what they think the other person thinks (inference — marked)
- what they believe the other person feels (inference — marked)
- what they are uncertain about (uncertainty — marked)

Inferred social states must never be written as objective facts.
"He was trying to intimidate me" is the POV's reading, not the truth —
unless the cognitive state establishes it as known.
